\title{What would establish a lower cost per surviving mangrove?\\ A finite-population audit of a restoration plan}
\author{Lluis Eriksson}
\date{19 November 2026}
\begin{document}\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
We use a proposed 20-hectare restoration budget in Muara Sekampung to ask what future observations would certify a lower initial cost per established plant. A verified count of 81,619 established plants from the self-managed nursery suffices even against perfect establishment of purchased seedlings. One of the five proposed configurations could supply this count alone. We obtain sharp finite-population error probabilities for audits that select the same number of positions but use different spatial sampling designs. Under a stated equal-capacity frame, 13 successes from 13 distinct random hectares give a worst-case false certification probability of 0.04882 or less. Thirteen successes from one random hectare can have probability above 0.75 under the same null. We derive an exact rule for unequal capacities and show separately how fixed costs affect the published density comparison. We also bound the power of all adaptive protocols limited by the number of hectares entered. Even with unrestricted effort within hectares, seven cannot attain 80\% power at complete establishment, whereas eight complete censuses can. These are design calculations for future monitoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{The decision and the missing observation}
Hafidhin, Kusmana and Rusdiana~\cite{plan} estimated the initial cost of two ways to provide the same $N=125{,}269$ seedlings. Their Table~8 gives $A=401{,}564{,}900$ Indonesian rupiah for a self-managed nursery and $B=616{,}326{,}000$ for external procurement. These totals include the same initial planting cost of 217,269,000 rupiah. Long-term maintenance and replacement lie outside the published budget. Their Table~7 assigns 102,960 positions to cluster planting over 16 hectares. Four other designs contain 4,356, 10,201, 5,151 and 2,601 positions. The authors explicitly describe proposed configurations awaiting field evaluation. Field research elsewhere has documented that planting targets and established plants can diverge~\cite{field}.

Fix one horizon, an establishment criterion and one identifiable original position per seedling. Let $X,Y$ denote the numbers established under the full nursery and purchasing scenarios. These are alternative outcomes for the same planning scale. For positive counts the nursery has a strictly lower initial cost per established plant exactly when $BX-AY>0$. We hold the two budgets fixed. The analysis does not estimate the procurement effect on survival. If a scenario produces no established plant its unit cost is undefined; a nursery with positive establishment still dominates zero output at positive cost.

\section{What a single census can certify}
Since $Y\leq N$, verifying $X>K=\lfloor AN/B\rfloor=81{,}618$ is sufficient against any purchasing outcome. It is necessary for a uniform conclusion if only a lower count for $X$ is known: at or below $K$ one can set $Y=N$ and $X$ to that count. A census confined to the four smaller designs can verify at most 22,309 establishments. Even full observation of both alternatives in those designs cannot certify an advantage uniformly: their best possible contribution is $B(22{,}309)$ and the unobserved large configuration can contribute $-A(102{,}960)$.

The large configuration alone can certify the sign. For example, if both full-scale scenarios yielded exactly $s$ establishments there, the worst remaining contribution is $-A(22{,}309)$. Then $(B-A)s>A(22{,}309)$ first holds at $s=41{,}714$. This two-scenario sensitivity calculation is distinct from the observable one-nursery threshold. We use the latter to construct a prospective test that requires access only to the implemented nursery.

\section{The spatial design changes the error rate}
Under the null $X\leq K$, a randomly audited position is scored as one only if establishment at the chosen horizon is verified. Unknowns must not be silently removed from the draw. Preselect the design and sample size and certify only when every sampled position scores one. All probability statements are over random selection from a fixed finite landscape. With a uniform sample without replacement from the full frame of $N$ positions, the maximal probability of certification under the null is $\binom Kk/\binom Nk$ at sample size $k$. The first $k$ at which it falls below 0.05 is seven. If only the $H=102{,}960$ positions in the large configuration are accessible, replace $N$ by $H$: the worst case places all $K$ survivors there. The first qualifying $k$ is 13. These fixed-sample guarantees cannot be reused at each of a sequence of chosen stopping times; time-uniform methods for finite populations are available~\cite{wor}.

For an explicit comparison suppose that the large configuration can be registered as $g=16$ blocks of $M=6{,}435$ positions each. The published planting density is an average; this equal-capacity register would need field verification. Write $x_j$ for the fixed number established in block $j$. The worst-case sum of the $x_j$ is $K$. Define $e_k$ as the sum of the products over all $k$-element subsets, with $e_0=1$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Choose $k$ distinct blocks uniformly, then independently choose one uniform position per selected block. The probability of all successes is $e_k(x)/[\binom gk M^k]$. It is maximized by balancing counts to within one. For two components $a,b$ with the others $z$, the numerator is $e_k(z)+(a+b)e_{k-1}(z)+ab\,e_{k-2}(z)$ (out-of-range terms are zero). Transferring a unit from the larger to the smaller when their difference exceeds one cannot decrease the product. The maximum is achieved with two counts of 5,102 and fourteen of 5,101.
\item Choose one block uniformly and sample $k$ distinct positions uniformly inside it. The probability of all successes is $\sum_j\binom{x_j}{k}/[g\binom Mk]$. The first difference of $\binom{x}{k}$ equals $\binom{x}{k-1}$ and increases weakly with $x$. Transferring a unit toward a larger non-full block cannot decrease this sum. The maximum is attained by twelve full blocks, one with 4,398 establishments, and three empty blocks.
\end{itemize}
For $k=13$ the two sharp probabilities are 0.048812 and 0.750441 respectively (rounded). The uniform position sample in the large configuration gives 0.048802. The concentrated design retains a false-certification probability of at least $12/16$ even if every position in the selected block is examined. These are upper error rates for the all-success rule, not estimates of its power. Dispersing visits can entail substantial travel costs which the monitoring calculation does not price.

\section{An exact rule for unequal block sizes}
Let the registered capacities be positive integers $m_1,\ldots,m_g$. Select a $k$-subset $I$ with probability $\prod_{i\in I}m_i/e_k(m)$ and then sample one uniform position from each selected block. The product-weight subset law is classical~\cite{weights}. Each admissible set of positions in distinct blocks is equally likely, giving the exact all-success probability $e_k(x)/e_k(m)$. Drawing successive blocks in proportion to remaining size need not implement the specified subset law. One implementation draws uniform $k$-subsets of positions and repeats the draw if two selected positions share a block, before observing any outcomes.

For total $K\leq\sum m_i$, find the largest integer $a\leq\max m_i$ with $\sum_i\min(m_i,a)\leq K$. Start at $x_i=\min(m_i,a)$, then distribute the remaining units among distinct unsaturated components. This vector maximizes the numerator: the pair transfer above increases balance whenever a smaller block has spare capacity and another exceeds it by at least two. The sum of squares strictly decreases at each transfer, so the procedure terminates at the described profile up to ties. All bounds are attained by actual integer configurations.

For $g=16$, fix the total $H=16M$, with each $m_i$ between $M-d$ and $M+d$. While $M-d\geq\lceil K/16\rceil$, the balanced maximizing numerator is feasible for every frame. The denominator is smallest at eight copies of each endpoint. Indeed, increasing the larger of two non-endpoint coordinates at the expense of the smaller cannot increase $e_k$. Iteration reaches endpoints, with at most one intermediate coordinate. The fixed sum forces that coordinate to an endpoint as well. Hence the two extremes are simultaneously attainable. Exact evaluation gives the largest symmetric tolerance for a uniform five-percent guarantee with 13 dispersed visits as \textbf{437 positions} on either side of 6,435. At the next integer the extremal frame fails. Wider tolerances include that same counterexample. For a known frame the direct ratio remains available.

\section{A separate sensitivity in the spending comparison}
Table~9 of the planning study compares costs at 5,000 seedlings per hectare. As an arithmetic normalization this scales the unit price. Interpreting it as expenditure at a new density additionally requires a model of which items change. Keep area fixed at 20 hectares and let $h=100{,}000/N$. If $F$ rupiah of the purchasing budget remain fixed and the rest scales proportionally, the hypothetical expenditure per hectare is $[hB+(1-h)F]/20$. It crosses the Table~9 reference of 24,879,600 rupiah per hectare at approximately 27.712 million rupiah of fixed costs. Holding only the 20 million planting transport cost fixed stays below this figure; holding only the 72 million planting labour cost fixed takes the re-budgeted estimate to 25.326 million rupiah per hectare. Both models match the original total exactly. These hypotheses illustrate sensitivity and do not identify actual contracts. The reference amount is attributed to the published table. Its administrative scope has not been independently established.

\paragraph{Scope and reproducibility.} The field paper supplies planning quantities and costs. No survival census, randomized audit, replacement policy or measured budget response is available for this proposed planting. A real audit needs a complete sampling frame, inclusion of failed positions, a fixed horizon and an unambiguous establishment criterion. Uncertain expenditure would also change $K$. The calculations use exact rational arithmetic. The companion files include the digitized quantities, all 32 selections of planting configurations, exhaustive small-frame checks of both spatial extremizers, and the two integer tolerance endpoints. No third-party article or field data are redistributed.
\section{How many hectares must an audit enter?}
A cap on the number of distinct hectares can bind even if measurement inside a visited hectare is free. Consider the 16-hectare stratum in isolation. Write $m_i$ for registered capacities and $K=81{,}618$. We require false certification probability at most $\alpha=1/20$ for every fixed field containing at most $K$ established plants. The alternative for this section is complete establishment throughout the accessible stratum. The procedure may inspect positions adaptively, revisit any observed block, randomize its decision and abstain. Its only information about establishment comes from its own inspections. We evaluate the number of distinct blocks entered. The sufficient procedures below can require a census of every position in each visited block.

For $g$ equal blocks of capacity $M$, let $q=\lfloor K/M\rfloor<g$ and $p={q\choose d}/{g\choose d}$, interpreting the numerator as zero for $d>q$. Among procedures entering at most $d$ blocks, the maximum probability of certification at complete establishment is
\[
  \beta_d=\begin{cases}\min(1,\alpha/p),&p>0,\\1,&p=0.\end{cases}
\]
To see necessity, fix the random choices of a procedure and follow the observations it would make in the completely established field. Make a null field by filling a uniform set of $q$ blocks and emptying the rest. If the visited blocks all belong to the filled set, the null field gives the same answers at every step. This event has probability at least $p$, including for paths that stop early. Averaging over the paths that certify in the full field, the mean null error is at least $p\beta_d$ for a procedure of power $\beta_d$. Conversely, draw $d$ distinct blocks uniformly and census every position in them. If all are established, certify with probability $\min(1,\alpha/p)$, or with probability one for $p=0$. No null field has more than $q$ fully established blocks, so the rule attains the upper bound.

Here $g=16$, $M=6{,}435$ and $q=12$. Seven visited blocks yield $p=9/130$ and power at most $13/18$. Eight give $p=1/26$ and allow power one. Thus eight is the minimum number of hectares for an audit with power at least $80\%$ under complete establishment. The argument bounds adaptive protocols as well as fixed designs. Eight complete censuses and thirteen single-position visits have different within-hectare costs.

\paragraph{Registered capacities.}
For unequal capacities let $\mathcal S$ contain every block set $S$ with $\sum_{i\in S}m_i\leq K$. The optimal power is the maximum of $\sum_I w_I$ over nonnegative weights on $d$-subsets, with $\sum_I w_I\leq1$ and $\sum_{I\subseteq S}w_I\leq\alpha$ for every $S\in\mathcal S$. To prove the upper bound, extend the set of blocks visited on every certifying trajectory of the full field to a $d$-set by any fixed rule. Its trajectory probability contributes to the weight of that set. A null field filling $S$ and emptying the complement reproduces every path whose extension lies inside $S$. To attain the bound, select $I$ with mass $w_I$, abstain with the remaining probability, and certify only if the selected blocks pass a full census. The set of fully established blocks of every null field belongs to $\mathcal S$.

Suppose the 16 capacities sum to $16M$ and differ from $M$ by at most $b$. The sum of any twelve is at most $12M+4b$. The sum of any thirteen is at least $13M-3b$. Thus for $b\leq678$ the feasible sets are exactly those of size at most twelve. At $b=679$ a thirteen-set is feasible precisely when its complement consists of three blocks all at $M+679$. Fourteen cannot fit, by the lower bound $14M-2b$. Let $t$ count blocks at the upper endpoint. The zero sum of deviations implies $t\leq8$. For $t<3$ the equal-capacity constraints still apply.

For $3\leq t\leq8$, symmetry of this family allows uniform sampling within the $t$ upper-endpoint blocks and the other $16-t$ blocks. For a footprint of eight hectares with $j$ selected from the first group, the fraction lying inside a null full set with $a$ and $h$ blocks of the respective groups is
\[
 \frac{{a\choose j}{h\choose 8-j}}{{t\choose j}{16-t\choose 8-j}}.
\]
The feasible null classes needed for the bound are every possible twelve-set and the class $(t-3,16-t)$. Minimize the largest inclusion probability over mixtures of feasible $j$. The exact optimal probabilities are shown below. The reproducibility files provide a rational design and a matching least-favourable null mixture for each value. Both bounds follow by multiplying the displayed matrix by the supplied probability vectors and checking entrywise inequalities.
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrr}
t&3&4&5&6&7&8\\ \hline
\text{probability}&35/906&70/1791&16/389&14/335&520/11533&65/1344
\end{array}
\]
Every entry is below $1/20$. The eight-hectare decision therefore survives this first change in the null family. Seven still cannot attain $80\%$, since the uniform mixture filling twelve blocks remains admissible. The bound on block visits depends on a prespecified horizon and the projected planting register. Its power at incomplete establishment can be much lower.

Equal-size omission probabilities and optimal linear programs over variable-size audit batches have precedents in election auditing~\cite{audit07,audit08}. The contribution here is the reduction of adaptive within-block observation to full-field trajectories for this decision, the resulting site-specific boundary, and exact certificates at the first exceptional capacities. No field survival or monitoring-cost observations enter these figures.
