# When does selecting forecasting views pay back its latency?

Choudhry et al. measured seven batch sizes (1, 10, 100, 500, 1,000, 2,000 and 10,000 forecasts) on one H100 and one CPU core. Their published setup used synthetic contexts representative of a median GIFT-Eval configuration. Table 8 gives base and cold times for two backtest-gated forecasting systems, batched warm and cold multipliers, and timing of a separate Toto-2-2.5B baseline. These measurements enable a question that a single throughput number cannot answer. How many successive batches guarantee lower total time when their sizes may change and the backtest selection can be reused?

**Decision and assumptions.** Compare serving every batch with the larger baseline against using xhigh Chronos-2 or TiRex-2 in this fixed configuration, with a cold first batch and warm subsequent batches. A cache hit reuses a selection suitable for that configuration. We use the tabulated times as deterministic service costs. Interleaving distinct configurations, changes in speed under sustained operation, additional invalidations, queue time, and prediction quality can all change the operational decision. In particular, this calculation imputes no accuracy to a cached prediction after a distribution shift. Table 7 reports better overall accuracy for the larger baseline than for xhigh Chronos-2 on GIFT-Eval.

**Forty Chronos-2 batches suffice; thirty-nine do not.** Let C(f), W(f) and T(f) be cold, warm and baseline time for batch size f. **The printed precision matters at the boundary.** We allow half a unit in the last printed decimal for each input, including the multipliers. Using only the separate time and warm columns gives a pessimistic threshold of 41. Incorporating the reported cold-to-base ratio links the admissible base and cold times. A base time B must lie in its own printed interval and in [C_low/R_high, C_high/R_low]. With this constraint the worst cold premium is at most 349/80 seconds; the smallest warm saving is at least 1273211/11380000 seconds. Thirty-nine reuses pay for startup throughout these intervals. Even at the favorable ends of the intervals, 38 reuses can still fall short. The resulting threshold is exactly 40 throughout the reported precision. These intervals express display precision only. Timing variation between runs could be larger than the margin.

**Model and workload matter.** For TiRex-2, repeating batches of 10,000 makes warm service slower than the larger baseline even at the favorable end of the display interval. No finite number of batches therefore suffices uniformly over the seven batch sizes. Restricted to sizes one and ten, the printed point values give 1,922 batches. Uncertainty induced by the printed digits permits thresholds ranging from 1,839 to 2,012 under the joint-column model.

**Can it be cheaper to wait before activating?** Allow the serving system to use Toto until any chosen batch, then activate Chronos once and reuse it. Comparing activation at batch i with deferring it to i+1 cancels all subsequent times. Earlier activation costs less whenever C(i)−T(i) < C(i+1)−W(i+1). Across all seven sizes and all jointly admissible printed values, the left side is at most 4.362500 seconds and the right side is at least 4.433000 seconds. Repeatedly moving the activation back by one batch proves that activating immediately dominates every delayed activation that eventually occurs. The minimum-time policy in hindsight is consequently one of two choices: use Toto throughout, or activate Chronos from the first batch. This result requires one valid selection across the sequence and additive service times. To certify the second bound, for each possible base time B we intersect the cold interval with [B R_low,B R_high], then minimize C−B W_ratio on this polygon; its breakpoints and endpoints suffice.
