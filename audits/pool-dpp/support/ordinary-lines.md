# Ordinary lines and exactness for real determinantal sampling

Analytic follow-up to the balanced-subset supplement. Assume a uniform proposal over d labels and equal inclusion probabilities for every (k-1)-subset. Keep the redraw rule. For a finite k-DPP with real Gram features and k>=3, the leading divergence coefficient vanishes if and only if the target is uniform on **all** k-subsets. In that case the sampler is exact at every N>=k. This implication does not require positive target mass on every subset as an extra assumption.

The sparse-support extension in [the companion note](sparse-support.md) proves that the coefficient at order N^(-2k) is c² Var_P(h)/2 even with zeros. If this coefficient vanishes then P is constant on its support because h_S=M P(S)-1. Consequently every (k-1)-subset has the same number L>0 of supported completions. For a Gram determinantal law a k-set has positive mass precisely when its vectors are linearly independent. In particular every (k-1)-set is independent.

Fix any k-3 labels C (empty if k=3) and pass to the quotient by their linear span. In the quotient, each pair of remaining labels gives two independent vectors. There is at least one independent triple, since C can be extended to a supported k-set. Every pair has exactly L third labels making an independent triple. If there are m=d-k+3 remaining labels, every projective line determined by a pair therefore contains precisely m-L labels, including the defining pair.

Here the real field matters. A finite noncollinear set of real projective points has a line containing just two of them (Sylvester–Gallai). To see the reduction from arbitrary dimension, choose a linear map to three dimensions preserving independence on all independent triples. Each bad determinant is the zero set of a nonzero polynomial in the map, and finitely many such sets cannot cover the real parameter space. Dependent triples remain dependent. Choose an affine chart containing all the image points. Among nonincident point and spanned-line pairs choose one with minimum positive distance D. If that line contains three points, two of them, A and B, lie on the same closed ray from the foot of the perpendicular, with B farther away. The distance from A to the line PB, with P the chosen off-line point, is |AB| D / |PB| < D. It is positive, a contradiction. This is the classical shortest-distance proof of the ordinary-line theorem.

Thus m-L=2 in the quotient, so every triple of remaining labels is independent. Taking any k-set and choosing C within it proves that the entire k-set was supported. Hence the original target is uniform on all k-subsets. Conversely this target is permutation invariant and is sampled exactly. The nine-label counterexample to exactness for unrestricted balanced subset laws consequently cannot be a real Gram k-DPP. Even without field-specific geometry, for that rank-three truncation the 36 pairs would have to be partitioned among six-point projective lines; 15 does not divide 36.

Classical geometry source: L. M. Kelly and W. O. J. Moser, [On the Number of Ordinary Lines Determined by n Points](https://doi.org/10.4153/CJM-1958-024-6), Canadian Journal of Mathematics (1958). The contribution claimed here is the application to the sampling coefficient; no priority claim is made for the incidence theorem. The shortest-distance argument uses real geometry. The complex representable case remains open in this research record.
