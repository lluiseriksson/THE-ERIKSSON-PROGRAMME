# What can past consumption order change in an otherwise identical electricity bill?

The primary sources are Emmanuel Kwezi Tabaro's Uganda SAM input and replication archive (20 November 2026, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24407802, CC BY 4.0) and the Electricity Regulatory Authority's domestic Code 10.1 schedule for October–December 2026. Its eligibility test uses the previous six months, excluding the month being billed. The financial comparison function in run_simulation.py takes a single argument, annual energy divided by twelve, and tests this value against 100 kWh. For a constant daily profile well away from the eligibility limit this shortcut can preserve the discount decision. It does not identify the charge under seasonal changes of load. The following is a counterfactual about the energy portion of a tariff. It uses no service charge, tax, actual grid connection or billed customer history. It makes no change to the source's simulated PV or battery outputs.

Let H be the fixed sequence of demands to be billed, each billing decision depending on whether the previous L periods total at most a cutoff. For a fixed billed month the discount is nonnegative, and its size depends on the current load, which is given. Suppose the multiset of preceding demands is known in each exchangeable calendar stratum but its order is unknown. Among admissible permutations, putting the largest demands earliest within each stratum minimizes every future trailing total; putting them latest maximizes every total. To prove this, consider one billed month whose window still contains past demands. Its historical portion is a suffix of the pre-billing sequence. Every stratum contributes a known number of slots to that suffix. The earliest-large allocation fills these slots with the smallest values of that stratum. This arrangement works for every suffix simultaneously. The fixed billed months in each trailing window make the same contribution to every arrangement. Once no past slots remain, the decisions coincide. Therefore the two sorted arrangements attain the minimum and maximum aggregate charge; monotonicity holds coordinate by coordinate. A continuous interval between the bounds need not be attainable.

For the source panel choose a low or a high archived daily load in each of the twelve 2026 calendar months. Read the actual rounded hourly CSV inputs, keep each complete 24-hour shape, and group months by their number of days. Group completed years by the exact twelve-month multiset of demands and by their exact October–December consumption sequence. Each demand identifies its daily shape and month length in this particular panel. Within each group the cost of current energy blocks is identical. Every eligible month saves exactly (779.4 − 250) × 15 = 7,941 UGX before other charges. For the 924 schedules with precisely six high months, only three schedules have any eligible month in Q4. Among 62 matched groups, exactly three allow bills with different energy charges. The largest difference is 7,941 UGX. These counts enumerate synthetic possibilities and have no probability interpretation.

For example, use high loads in February, July, August, September, November and December versus January, February, March, April, November and December. These years share the same monthly demand multiset and the same October, November and December inputs. November is eligible only in the second. The preceding six-month totals are 735.9816 and 551.9816 kWh respectively. A summary that takes the annual mean would give both schedules the same answer. The annual-mean simplification alone does not establish a reversal of the archived scenarios' relative costs.

Files calendar.json and order.json contain the charged totals, inclusive threshold comparisons and witness schedules. The former evaluates the six-high design; the latter checks the theorem on all 4,096 low/high years.
