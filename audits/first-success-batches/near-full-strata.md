# The upper endpoint through two failures beyond the cap

With the same first-hit score and the same independent common-cap design, the stratum comparison \(E[Z1_{\{S=k\}}]\le\Pr(S=k)\) holds for every \(m,n\ge2\) and every \(k\ge m\) with \(f=mn-k\le n+2\). For \(f<n\) it is an equality. For cap two it also follows from the earlier full-tail result. No restriction on the task probabilities is required.

Group full paths by the unordered profile of *failures* rather than successes, using odds \((1-p_i)/p_i\) in the symmetrized monomial comparison. For interior probabilities, remove the common factor \(\prod_i p_i^n\); extend the final inequality by continuity. Paths with no exhausted task have \(Z=w_s\ge1\) and demand the excess \(w_s-1\) above their unit full mass. Paths with an exhausted task have \(Z=0\) and supply unit mass. Homogeneous coefficient matching makes total supply equal total demand at fixed \(k\ge m\). A supply profile can compensate a demand profile whenever it majorizes it. For \(f<n\) every path is all-hit and \(s\le f<n\), hence has weight one. For \(f=n\) the only supply profile is \((n,0,\ldots)\); for \(f=n+1\) it is \((n,1,0,\ldots)\). Either majorizes every possible demand profile.

Take \(f=n+2\) and \(n\ge3\). The supply profiles, padded by zeros, are \((n,2)\) and \((n,1,1)\). The former majorizes every demand. The latter majorizes every demand with at least three nonempty failure slots: the largest count is at most \(n-1\), the largest two sum to at most \(n+1\), and the largest three sum to at most \(n+2\). It suffices to pay the two-slot demand from the two-slot supply, leaving all remaining transfers feasible.

Fix the two slots. The number of supplying paths for these slots is \(2\binom n2\), and no other task has a failure. If \(n=3\), two all-hit slots can hold at most four failures and the two-slot demand vanishes. For \(n\ge4\), consider the experiment restricted to these two tasks. It has \(2n-f=n-2\ge2\) successes, and the entire supply and demand in that stratum are supported on precisely those two slots. Homogeneous calibration shows that their total excess demand is exactly \(2\binom n2\). Restoring the \(m-2\) perfect logs leaves each wait total and each labelled path multiplicity unchanged, while weakly decreasing each score weight \(w_s\).

For that last fact, \(1/w_s\) is the probability a uniform weak composition of \(s\) failures into the given number of parts has all parts below \(n\). Represent a uniform composition with \(m+1\) parts as a uniform word with \(s\) stars and \(m\) bars. Deleting a uniformly chosen bar yields a uniform word with one fewer bar (every shorter word has the same number of marked insertions). A merge preserving the cap requires that the two original parts already respected it. Thus cap compliance weakly increases with the number of parts, proving \(w_s^{(m)}\le w_s^{(2)}\) whenever the two-part denominator is nonzero. This proves the required two-slot bound and completes the transport argument.

The coefficients through \(f=n+2\) concern fixed total counts at arbitrary probabilities. The programme's earlier uniform-high-reliability.md bounds the sign of the full tail in a neighborhood of equal certainty. The two statements answer different questions. For \(f=n+3\), controlling three-slot demand simultaneously with two-slot demand requires an additional argument.
